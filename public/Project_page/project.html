Abstract

Although cancer incidence increases with country income, past studies suggest that   lower and middle income countries (LMICs) have significantly higher mortality, with approximately 75% of cancer deaths occurring in LMICs. In high income countries (HICs), patient tumours and body fluids are routinely sent for genomic screening,  informing physicians of actionable drug targets and residual disease. However, these innovations are inaccessible to LMICs due to the prohibitive costs of genome sequencing facilities, contributing to increased mortality.

Here, we demonstrate preliminary evidence of a novel, low-cost, RNA oligomer-based cancer subtyping tool in detecting the relative abundance of disparate mRNA transcripts. We used NUPACK and ViennaRNA to design RNA oligomers which use homologous strand displacement to adopt specific conformations based on the relative abundance of vimentin (VIM) and cadherin-1 (CDH1) mRNAs. Notably, these conformations can be detected using inexpensive polyacrylamide (PAGE) gels and widely available nucleic acid staining, providing the basis for a low-cost cancer subtyping tool. Our findings demonstrate the potential of RNA oligomer nanostructures in the resource-limited diagnostic settings.

Project Background

Cancer outcomes are strongly associated with a country's income level. Compared to Low- and Middle- income countries (LMICs), High income countries (HICs) generally have higher cancer incidence and lower mortality rate relative to LIC/LMICs. The high incidence observed in HICs is highly attributable with their older populations, as cancer risk increases substantially with age and the median age of all cancer type diagnoses is approximately 66 years (Bray et al., 2024; National Cancer Institute, 2025 ). In contrast, the poorer survival rates of cancers in LIC/LMICs is associated with limited access to screening and inadequate access to appropriate treatments and care (Martinez et al., 2024; World Health Organization, 2026). All three of these disparities are severely exacerbated by limited access to molecular diagnostics, which have become indispensable components of how HICs manage cancers (Mosele et al., 2024; Kazmi et al., 2025).

Molecular diagnostics can be broadly categorized as those which are used for either subtyping or detection purposes. In the subtyping application, a biopsy is taken from a newly diagnosed patient and sent for assay. The goal is to determine the specific genotypic characteristics of the cancer, helping inform physicians of suitable therapeutic options. All therapeutic efficacy is influenced by tumour genotype, especially molecularly targeting drugs and certain immunotherapies, which rely on targeting specific cancer-derived mutant proteins (neoantigens). Thus, physicians’ ability to accurately determine the tumour genotype influences the therapies they can prescribe, thus precluding LIC/LMIC cancer patients to new molecular targeted therapies (REF). The detection application refers to the use of molecular diagnostics to detect residual tumour cells. Most (~90%) of cancer deaths are attributed to metastasis that arises from tumours which survive treatment and regrow over months or years. Thus, when a cancer patient has completed a treatment course, physicians in HICs, in addition to physical examination, will obtain a liquid biopsy, such as a blood draw, from the patient (Mosele et al., 2024). Then, using mainly sequencing technologies, they can determine the extent of residual disease by detecting cancer cell-specific cell-free DNA. These two approaches have been widely adopted in HIC oncology regimens, to immense clinical success. In a systematic review  of 9,819 cases by Kazmi and colleagues, patients receiving sequencing-informed subtyping targeted therapy had a 34% lower hazard of progression, compared to patients receiving standard therapy (Kazmi et al., 2025). Likewise, systematic review of 80 clinical trial studies reveal a 7.48-fold higher hazard of recurrence for patients testing positive for circulating tumour DNA compared to those testing negative after treatment (Zheng et al., 2024). Therefore, molecular diagnostics are an essential component of effective modern oncology management.

Unfortunately, molecular diagnostics remain largely inaccessible to cancer patients in LIC/LMICs. This is primarily attributed to the considerable, and often prohibitive financial and infrastructure requirements of many conventional molecular diagnostics platforms, particularly next generation sequencing (NGS) based assays. NGS, commonly performed using Illumina short-read sequencing platforms, has become a major technology for clinical cancer genomic profiling because of its high throughput and comparatively low cost per sequenced base. Nevertheless, NGS remains prohibitively costly and thus out of reach to LMICs, due to the immense up-front capital investment, computational requirements, and constant need for highly trained personnel (Chaudhry & Ehtesham, 2026).

Today, cancer diagnostics rely on several conventional testing platforms. Three of the dominant technologies are listed below. Most require expensive instruments and specialized bioinformaticians to process the data which many LIC/LMICs do not have the resources or organizational expertise to support.

  1. Targeted Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS)

(e.g., Illumina NextSeq, TruSight Panels):

  • How it works: A sequencing instrument extracts genomic DNA or RNA from a tissue biopsy and reads the nucleic acid sequence letter-by-letter using fluorescent imaging. Powerful bioinformatics pipelines then align millions of reads to detect specific mutations (Illumina, 2024).  
  1. Deep Cell-Free DNA Sequencing / Liquid Biopsies

(e.g., Guardant360, Galleri):

  • How it works: Highly sensitive sequencing platforms search peripheral blood draws for circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) fragments shed by dividing or residual cancer cells (US Food and Drug Administration, 2026; (GRAIL, n.d).

  1. Protein Biomarker Tests 

(e.g., OncoSeek, CA-125, CA19-9):

  • How it works: Standard laboratory immunoassays use antibodies to detect soluble proteins or tumor antigens shed into patient serum (Luan et al., 2023)

Targeting the relative abundance of CDH1 and VIM provides two key advantages for tumor subtyping:

1. Ideal Complementary Triggers

Across diverse cancer cell lines, pure epithelial-like phenotypes express CDH1 at levels roughly 10-fold higher than VIM, while mesenchymal-like cells express VIM roughly 10-fold higher than CDH1(REF). Furthermore, a strong correlation between CDH1/VIM mRNA and protein expression ratios in lung adenocarcinoma cell lines, supporting the idea that transcript abundance is an indicator of the epithelial-mesenchymal phenotype (Schliekelman et al., 2015). High CDH1 signals an adherent epithelial state (Conformation Z), high VIM indicates an invasive mesenchymal state (Conformation X), and intermediate levels of both capture the plastic hybrid transition state (Conformation Y)

2. Clinical Sufficiency in Solid Tumors (Li et al., 2025)

In gastric cancer, gene expression correlation analysis demonstrated that the combined ratio of just CDH1 and VIM is sufficient to stratify patient samples into distinct epithelial (EPC), hybrid (HPC), and mesenchymal (MPC) subtypes, which correlate directly with tumor infiltration, metastasis, and clinical survival outcomes.

Goals

Design Goals

  1. Identify suitable target regions of CDH1 and VIM, which are representative of those unlikely to be protein-bound and thus easily accessed by the detector.
  2. Evaluate thermodynamic stability of possible detector strand sequences and their complementarity to assess binding stability.
  3. Cross-reference candidate sequences against gene annotations to ensure biological relevance of finalized sequences.
  4. Design oligomer detector architecture and evaluate candidate structures for predicted conformations.
  5. Evaluate robustness of oligomer detector design when exposed to alternative sequence position, nucleotide composition, and fragment length.

Experimental Goals

  1. Protocol 1. Demonstrate successful hybridization of the target VIM and CDH1 fragments and the corresponding sequence on the detector.
  2. Protocol 2. Demonstrate conformational change of the detector in the presence of VIM and CDH1, resulting in the predicted differential migration pattern.
  3. Protocol 3. Demonstrate predicted migration of the detector when exposed to a varying titration of VIM and CDH1 levels.

Objectives

  1. Research and develop a nucleic acid oligomer capable of distinguishing the relative abundance of VIM and CDH1, two markers indicative of mesenchymal and epithelial cell states, respectively.
  2. Establish whether RNA hybridization and structural change produces distinguishable migration differences which can be resolved on native PAGE.
  3. Establish preliminary proof of concept for a relative RNA abundance assay by determining if varying VIM:CDH1 ratios produces measurable differences in gel migration patterns.

Feasibility

Our design is an RNA-based oligomer which forms distinct conformations based on hybridization to one, the other, or both target mRNAs, whose conformation is then detected based on its relative migration on a native PAGE gel. The principle of conformation-dependent oligomer migration, that is, oligomers of the same molecular weight migrating differently based on their steric hindrance, has been demonstrated by Heuer and colleagues (Heuer et al., 2004). Principles of nucleic acid hybridization are broadly understood and are routinely modelled computationally, while our experiments relied on accessible and rudimentary gel electrophoresis.

Computationally, The feasibility of our project was supported by the availability of established RNA secondary structural prediction and modelling computational modelling tools such as NUPACK, ViennaRNA, as well as our custom “sliding window” algorithm, which enabled us to evaluate the thermodynamic behaviour and characteristics of candidate sequences. The widespread use of these tools allowed us to iterate possible detector designs in silico, bypassing expensive benchtop-based screening experiments.

The design of our detector was very straightforward in its implementation. Consisting of a single 46 nucleotide single stranded RNA, targeting two 20-nucleotide target fragments, all of the required nucleic acids could be commercially synthesized and used with no prior assembly steps. By using an oligomer approach, we limited the number of components needing to be prepared, validated, and optimized.

Finally, the experimental methods required to evaluate the design are rudimentary, and fast. RNA annealing and native PAGE provided a flexible means of investigating differences in conformational change via electrophoretic migration without requiring special equipment or assays.

Challenges

The first challenge was identifying suitable targets. This was mainly due to the tendency for RNA to interact with itself and other species, fragment specificity, and manufacturing constraints. RNA forms secondary structures and interacts with proteins, which physiologically, can interfere with complementary sequence hybridization. We needed a way to gather enough information on the nature of these interactions, predicting an optimal sequence to represent VIM and CDH1, which was as free of these binding hindrances as possible. Fragment specificity was another concern. Given the limitations imposed upon us by the maximum length of single stranded RNA oligomer that is commercially manufactured, being about 100 nucleotides, and furthermore the high costs of fragments exceeding 20 base pair fragments to represent our VIM and CDH1. Thus, achieving specificity in the RNA fragments’ representation of their respective sequences was of large concern. Although our computational sliding window approach allowed us to identify the most favourable sequences with respect to secondary structure formation, their accessibility within biological samples remains uncertain.

The second challenge was designing the detector. To design a single RNA strand which was capable of adopting distinct conformations based on VIM, CDH1, or both, was theoretically and computationally challenging. In addition to favouring hybridization with either target, the detector also needed to form structures with sufficiently distinct geometries to produce distinguishable migration patterns on native PAGE. This required evaluating several candidate architectures. Although these tools allowed us to predict the structures formed under different conditions, the actual conformations and behaviour in electrophoresis could not be established computationally alone. We speculate that should this concept be further developed, machine learning algorithms could provide immense assistance in the design and screening of future detector strands, either further optimized for VIM and CDH1 detection, or other combinations of biomarkers.

Our third challenge was with respect to determining the ideal conditions to obtain the designed conformational changes in the lab. Though protocol 1 demonstrated hybridization between the target fragments and their complement, our initial attempts with the complete detector did not produce distinguishable migration patterns (Fig A2). The investigation of suitable ionic conditions, annealing temperature, and target-to-detector stoichiometry was time consuming and ultimately prevented us from completing protocol 3. These results suggest that experimental conditions, particularly ionic strength and temperature, were extremely important for obtaining the intended behaviour. Unfortunately, due to technical difficulties with gel casting and imaging, we were unable to complete experiment 3 in the available time. Thus, our results provide preliminary evidence of target-dependent electrophoretic behaviour, but the ability of the detector to distinguish relative VIM and CDH1 abundance remains untested.

A control conditions gel, gel 1, was loaded with an unbuffered detector and incubated at 94C for 2 minutes to reflect baseline sample preparation conditions adopted from protocol 1. The detector loaded in gel 2 was buffered with 333mM sodium acetate and incubated at 37C for 2 minutes, while the detector loaded in gel 3 was instead incubated at 94C for 2 minutes. Only the conditions of gel 3, at the 2:1 target (VIM or CDH1) to the detector produced a distinguishable electrophoretic mobility shift between the VIM and CDH1 conditions. All gels are 8% acrylamide:bisacrylamide, were ran for approximately 30 minutes at 100V, then stained with 1:10,000 SYBR Gold in running buffer for 30 minutes.
