Contamination control procedures:

We remained cognizant of nuclease contamination at all times by working on a workbench set aside for nuclease-free work, decontaminating the equipment and workspace with DNA-destroying and nuclease inactivating agents before any work was done, and using filter tips in our micropipettes. We explicitly used nuclease free reagents and consumables whenever they were expected to come into direct contact with our RNAs.

For the gels and running buffers, we did not employ additional RNase decontamination procedures. Given the distinct bands observed during the initial attempts at Protocol 0, and the absence of visible evidence of RNA fragmentation, we concluded that substantial RNA degradation during electrophoresis was unlikely.

Protocol 1: Validating successful hybridization between complementary strands of detector and VIM/CDH1 targets

The purpose of this experiment was to demonstrate that the complementary sequences of the detector could hybridize with their respective target mRNA fragments under the annealing conditions used in subsequent experiments. By demonstrating successful hybridization under these conditions, we could establish that the designed sequences were capable of binding, helping to rule out intrinsic complement-target incompatibility as a potential cause of failure in later experiments.

Method

  1. A 15% Acrylamide gel was cast using the reagents described in Protocol 0.
  2. In equal parts, 200uM VIM and VIM Complement, as well as CDH1 and CDH1 Complement were mixed together (Tubes 1 and 2, experimental samples).
  3. An equal volume of VIM, VIM complement, CDH1, and CDH1 complement alone were aliquoted (Tubes 3, 4, 5, and 6, single strand controls).
  4. All tubes containing the nucleic acids were heated at 94 Celsius for 2 minutes to promote denaturing of existing structures and reannealing between complement and target.
  5. After the tubes were allowed to cool to room temperature, 6X TriTrack DNA Loading Dye was added.
  6. Each sample was loaded into the 15% Acrylamide gel with a final volume of 12uL per sample (absolute total mRNA quantity of 2000pmol) and ran at 100V for 30 minutes.
  7. After the run, the gel was incubated in 1:10,000 SYBRGold diluted in a running buffer for 30 minutes, whilst protected from light.
  8. The gel was imaged with autoexposure under the SYBRGold filter.

Results & Discussion

For both VIM and CDH1, the complement and the actual RNA itself are both 20 nucleotides long. Given that complement anneals to the target, this would create a double stranded species with a weight approximately equivalent to 40 nucleotides (Fig. 1A). Thus, a successfully annealed complement and target would be heavier, and thus expected to run at a slower rate on a native PAGE gel compared to individual VIM and CDH, or if the complement and target did not anneal to each other successfully. For both CDH1 and VIM, we demonstrate successful annealing between it and its complement (Fig. 1B). The differential migration between the target and complement only conditions may reflect differences in secondary structure formation. This is supported by the repeated observation of CDH1 complement migrating slower than CDH1, as well as VIM complement migrating faster than VIM in previous attempts (Fig. A1).

Protocol 2: Conformational change of whole detector in response to VIM or CDH1

The purpose of this experiment was to demonstrate that the detector strand changes conformation when bound to VIM and CDH1, and that these changes in conformation can be resolved between detector only (no target), detector and VIM, and detector CDH1. We compared 37°C, the predicted ideal hybridization temperature from our computational modelling, and 94°C, the recommended annealing temperatures suggested by the oligomer manufacturer. Additionally, given that RNA secondary structures have been known to require an environment of high ionic strength to form, we compared a NFH₂O only to a 333mM sodium acetate solution condition.

Method

  1. An 8% native polyacrylamide gel (19:1 acrylamide:bis-acrylamide) was cast using 1X running buffer
  2. 10X folding buffer was prepared, consisting of 410mg of Sodium Acetate Anhydrous in 1.5mL of NFH₂O
  3. Reaction mixtures were prepared in NFH₂O to a final volume of 10 µL, testing four stoichiometric ratios of detector strand to target mRNA (VIM or CDH1):
  • 1:1 Ratio: 0.5 µL detector (200 µM) + 0.5 µL Target mRNA (VIM or CDH1) + 1 µL 10X Buffer + 8 µL NFH₂O.
  • 1:2 Ratio: 0.5 µL detector + 1.0 µL Target mRNA + 1 µL 10X Buffer + 7.5 µL NFH₂O.
  • 1:3 Ratio: 0.5 µL detector + 1.5 µL Target mRNA + 1 µL 10X Buffer + 7.0 µL NFH₂O.
  • 2:1 Ratio: 1.0 µL detector + 0.5 µL Target mRNA + 1 µL 10X Buffer + 7.5 µL NFH₂O.
  1. Samples were incubated at 94°C for 2 minutes.
  2. Following incubation, 2 µL of 6X TriTrack DNA Loading Dye was added to each tube.
  3. The samples were loaded into the 8% gel alongside a low-molecular-weight RNA ladder (10 µL The per lane) and ran at 100V for 30 minutes.
  4. The gel was incubated in 1:10,000 SYBRGold diluted in a running buffer for 30 minutes, whilst protected from light.
  5. Gel was imaged on a transilluminator under the SYBR Gold filter with autoexposure.

Results and Discussion

By exposing the structure to either CDH1 or VIM at different stoichiometries, we aimed to elicit the designed conformational shift in the detector, whose differences in bulkiness could result in detectable differences in native PAGE migration (Fig. 2A). Indeed, we observe that only at the 2:1 target VIM or CDH1 to detector ratios, that there is an observable difference in the relative migration of the CDH1 detector structure (Conformation Z) and the VIM detector structure (Conformation X) (Fig 2B). This is also only observed in the samples incubated in the 333mM sodium acetate buffer, annealed at 94C for 2 minutes (Fig. A2). In the gels where the expected migration pattern is not observed, the presence of dual bands are thought to correspond to the 46 base pair detector strand (the upper, thus slower migrating band), and the 20 base pair target strand (Fig. A2).

Figure 2.  Differential migration of stoichiometric ratio of 2:1 target mRNA fragment to detector suggests successful adoption of intended conformational change. A) Schematic depicting experimental workflow and expected confirmation of the detector in response to CDH1 or VIM. B) At the 2:1 target (VIM or CDH1) to detector ratio, the putative CDH1 conformation, depicted in A, achieved a further migration than the putative VIM conformation. This is not observed at any other stoichiometries of target (VIM or CDH1) to detector ratios.

It is well known that RNA conformational changes depend on an environment with the appropriate ionic strength, as cations help shield the electrostatic repulsion between negatively charged phosphate groups of the RNA backbone, thereby stabilizing secondary and tertiary structures formed by RNA (Draper, 2008). Additionally, we speculate that the 94C, but not 37C incubation temperature resulted in the desired conformational change as 94C heating likely disrupted existing secondary structures, with subsequent cooling permitting new structures to form, while the 37C heating was insufficient to overcome existing secondary structures, resulting in a greater number of detectors failing to anneal with the target VIM or CDH1 (Tinoco & Bustamante 1999).

Results and Discussion

This experiment was not performed within the available period due to technical issues with gel casting and imaging.

Overall Conclusion

We designed and investigated an RNA-based detector intended to distinguish relative abundance of VIM and CDH1 transcripts through conformational changes detected by differential migration on gel electrophoresis. Using computational modelling, we identified suitable target sequences, developing a 46 nucleotide detector strand predicted to adopt three distinct configurations depending on presence of VIM, CDH1, or both targets.

To this end, we demonstrated experimentally the hybridization between the target VIM and CDH1 fragments and their complements found on the detector. We further observed distinct migration patterns when the complete detector was incubated with either VIM or CDH1 under optimized annealing conditions. However, these conformations remain putative; the exact conformation has yet to be verified.

Though the results support the feasibility of our proposed detection mechanism, we could not complete the experiments necessary to establish the detector’s ability to respond to relative levels of VIM and CDH1 abundance.

Thus, our findings support the use of RNA hybridization and conformation dependent electrophoretic migration as the basis for a low-cost molecular detection system.
