Future Directions

Future directions of this project were grouped into three categories:characterization experiments, investigation of additional biomarkers/cancer types, and workflow development.

Characterization

Although our experiments demonstrate preliminary detection activity, an immediate next step would be to further characterize its quantitative performance by creating standard calibration curves, determining limits of detection, precision, and performance with actual biological samples as opposed to the manufactured oligomers used here.

Many characteristics of actual samples will differ from our oligomer conditions which could directly affect the folding of our detector. For instance, we demonstrate in Protocol 2 that the detector folds in approximately 333mM ionic conditions, but not in pure water. How the presence of other contaminants, and thus the extent of sample preparation needed, will affect the detector’s activity remain to be determined.

Investigation of additional biomarkers/cancer types

The ability of this detector to discriminate between the abundance of two markers has utility across many cancers by which a select pair or trio of markers strongly affects the types of treatments administered. For instance, breast cancer treatment decisions are often informed by multigene expression assays such as Oncotype DX, while similar classifiers have been developed for prostate cancer and others. Although these technologies assess a substantially larger panel of genes than our detector, they demonstrate the clinical utility of measuring gene expression. Thus, future efforts could investigate using alternative pairs of biomarker mRNA pairs to assess other cancers, designing new detectors for other cancer types and expanding our concept’s overall molecular subtyping applications. Additional efforts could also explore the possibility of developing machine learning models to automatically devise and formulate future detector strands for new biomarkers.

Workflow development

Finally, future efforts could investigate the streamlining and optimization of the experimental workflow to further improve the accessibility of the detector to the intended low-resource settings. Although the detector already consists of relatively inexpensive RNA oligomers, the current workflow requires specialized equipment and reagents for electrophoresis, staining, and imaging, in addition to special RNAse-contamination aware procedures required. These may limit its practical applicability in LIC and LMICs for which it was designed. Thus, future investigations could explore simplified RNA extraction and purification methods, a simplified PAGE detection apparatus to reduce reagent consumption and increase throughput, and to lower the need for RNAse-contamination aware protocols. These improvements would reduce the overall cost and infrastructure requirements of the platform.
