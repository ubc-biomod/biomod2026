Ethical, Legal, and Social Issues

Goal of the ELSI:

The purpose of an ELSI is to consider all audiences, people and organizations, which may be affected by our project.

Recap of project:

Our team is developing an RNA-based mRNA Abundance Detector that is designed to bind differentially depending on the relative amounts of VIM and CDH1 ctRNA. Both genes are markers of epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) which is a process linked to how cancer cells behave. Our experiments test whether the components bind their intended RNA targets and whether the resulting shapes present differently on a polyacrylamide gel (PAGE).  

1. Stakeholders and impact pathways

The people most directly affected are the researchers, laboratory technicians and supervisors who need reproducible results and have to handle reagents and waste safely. However, patient and tissue donors would need to understand how their samples and health information are used. Clinicians and pathologists would want evidence that a result means something clinically before relying on it, and hospitals, ethics boards and regulators would look closely at consent, patient safety and quality control. Manufacturers and business partners would want clear information about reproducibility and intellectual property, while health systems, particularly those with limited resources, would care about the full cost of testing rather than the price of the gel alone.

2. Core ethical, legal, and social issues

2.1 Clinicians and patients - Clinical validity, patient safety, and avoiding overclaiming

The main concern here is that a VIM/CDH1 signal could be mistaken for a complete picture of a tumour. Both genes are associated with Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) cells, but cancer cells can sit in intermediate or hybrid states, and expression of these genes shifts with biological context (Pastushenko et al., 2018; Schliekelman et al., 2015; Yang et al., 2020). Thus, even if the RNA binds exactly as designed, a gel result would not tell us a tumour's subtype, its prognosis or the right treatment.

The observed result could also shift with RNA concentration, sample preparation and how the bands are read. A future iteration of this design could be used without adequate validation which could produce an inaccurate reading, leading to unnecessary follow-up, delayed care or a poor treatment decision. Diagnostic accuracy studies need suitable reference methods and clear reporting of uncertainty to prevent this (Bossuyt et al., 2015).

Potential responses from non-researchers

Oncologists and pathologists are unlikely to adopt a new method that has never been compared with established tests. Patients who assumed that a research-stage gel result was a reliable diagnosis but later learned it was not, would have good reason to lose trust. Additionally, harm would be greater if that result had influenced their treatment.

How we would address this

We will report the results of what the PAGE experiments show and run appropriate positive and negative controls. Before clinical use, there would need to be a clinical trial for the specific helper conducted. Additionally, there may also be reproducibility experiments or tests to validate results and efficacy, thresholds set in advance and comparison against an established test or suitable reference method.

2.2 Patients and tissue donors - Informed consent and governance of tumour tissue

If later work used tumour biopsies or stored human tissue, donors need to be told and made sure that they  understand that their samples were helping to test an experimental system, not producing a diagnosis for them. Consent information should cover the purpose of the study, the possible risks, any linked health records, how samples are handled, and whether individual results will be returned (CIOMS, 2016; World Medical Association, 2024).

Tissue collected for clinical care cannot be assumed to be available for any research purpose. Using it for something new without renewed consent would need an appropriate ethics basis, and donors should be told what withdrawal can and cannot achieve once data are de-identified or analyses are complete (World Medical Association, 2016).

Potential responses from non-researchers

Patients could decline if the purpose or future use of their tissue is unclear. Additionally, if samples were used outside an approved agreement, patients could complain, withdraw or lose trust in the institution, and an ethics board could suspend the study.

How we would address this

Before using any human samples, we would obtain the required ethics and biosafety approvals local to that region and adhering to international standards, explain participation in plain language, and document either consent or an approved exception. We would also set out in advance how specimens are stored, shared and withdrawn from the study.

2.3 Patients - Privacy, confidentiality, and data minimization

The gel and detection currently responds to only two mRNA transcripts, VIM and CDH1, and does not produce anyone's full genetic profile. Privacy still matters if future research links a result to a named patient, a biopsy record or a treatment history, because that combination could reveal sensitive details about someone's cancer care even without any genome sequencing (CIOMS, 2016; World Medical Association, 2016).

Removing names from a dataset is also not the same as making identification impossible. If a coded sample can be traced back to a patient, the code and the records attached to it still need protection.

Potential responses from non-researchers

Patients may refuse to provide samples if their data could be shared without clear limits. Hospitals and ethics boards would likely insist on stronger protections, and a breach could put people off joining later studies.

How we would address this

We would collect only the information needed for validation, label samples with coded IDs, store the identifying key separately and limit access to approved personnel. Any sharing or secondary use would follow institutional requirements and the terms of the consent or ethics approval.

2.4 Patients and health systems - Equity, affordability, and meaningful access

Part of our reason for exploring PAGE is that it can be less resource-intensive than some more complex molecular methods. But an inexpensive gel does not make an entire clinical test affordable. Human samples would still need to be collected, RNA would need to be prepared, and the work would require suitable equipment, trained staff, quality control and possibly confirmatory testing. Costs like these are a major barrier to diagnostic access, particularly where laboratory infrastructure is limited (Fleming et al., 2021).

It would be ethically troubling to describe the method as accessible if it only works reliably in well-equipped laboratories. Nor should lower-resource settings be expected to accept weaker evidence of accuracy just because more advanced tests are harder for them to obtain.

Potential responses from non-researchers

Hospitals and public funders would reasonably question the claimed savings if preparation, equipment and repeat testing turn out to be expensive, and patients would be let down if a technology advertised as widely accessible were unavailable locally.

How we would address this

We would compare the cost per usable result, failed tests included, against suitable existing methods. A future clinical version would also need to be tested in the settings where it is meant to be used, with a realistic plan for training and maintenance.

2.5 Hospitals, regulators and manufacturers - Regulatory pathways and truthful intended-use claims

If the specific project were ever developed into a test that informs cancer care, its developers would first have to define exactly what the gel  results can meaningfully show. Clinical tests must meet requirements for evidence, safety, quality and manufacturing consistency that vary by jurisdiction, and our current PAGE prototype does not meet them simply by producing a laboratory signal (WHO, 2023; IMDRF, 2024).

Potential responses from non-researchers

Hospitals and regulators can be expected to reject a product whose claims go beyond its evidence, and manufacturers would hesitate to invest without a consistent process and a clearly defined use. Patients, meanwhile, could be misled if a research prototype were promoted as an approved cancer test or treatment.

How we would address this

We should make clear that right now,  the project is a research-stage prototype. Any later diagnostic or therapeutic product would need its own clinical validation, manufacturing controls and regulatory review before being offered to patients.

4.6 Laboratory staff and waste handlers - Biosafety, chemical safety, and waste management

Our PAGE experiments need laboratory safety precautions even though no human tissue is involved. Unpolymerized acrylamide in particular is hazardous and can be absorbed through the skin, and its risks differ from those of a finished, polymerized gel (NIOSH, 1989). The stains and other reagents we use should each be assessed against their safety data sheets.

If human tumour material is introduced later, its biological hazards would need to be assessed separately from the chemical hazards of making the gel. WHO guidance calls for risk-based handling, decontamination and proper waste segregation (WHO, 2020a, 2020b).

Potential responses from non-researchers

Laboratory staff and waste handlers would reasonably raise concerns if the procedures for handling chemicals, biological material or waste were unclear. An exposure incident or improper disposal could injure people, halt experiments and damage public trust.

How we would address this

We will follow current safety data sheets and institutional procedures, wear the required protective equipment, and separate and label waste appropriately. Any work involving human tissue would first need an updated risk assessment and additional training.

4.7 Researchers and businesses - Intellectual property, open science, and access-conscious commercialization

Our proposed solution builds on published methods for nucleic-acid sensing, strand displacement and gel electrophoresis. If the project were ever commercialized, we would have to check whether any sequences, software, design features or methods are covered by patents or licence conditions.

Licensing choices would also shape who can use the technology. An exclusive agreement might help fund further development, but it could restrict which laboratories are able to manufacture or use a future test (WHO, WIPO & WTO, 2020).

Potential responses from non-researchers

Collaborators and companies are likely to be reluctant to work with a design whose ownership or licensing is unclear, and disputes could delay development. Patients, too, could fairly question the value of an “affordable” technology if licensing later makes it hard to obtain.

How we would address this

We would keep a record of where our sequences, software and design methods come from and under what licences. Anyone attempting commercialization should seek institutional advice on ownership and freedom to operate, and look for licensing options that do not restrict access more than necessary.

4.8 Patients and the public - Public communication, trust, and adoption

A gel with several bands can look like a simple way to classify cancers. In this project, though, the bands only show differences in the shape of a RNA construct under controlled conditions. There is no evidence that a patient's tumour has been identified or that a drug would work.

Potential responses from non-researchers

Members of the public may be interested in the potential applications but will be skeptical if our assay claims to show something it cannot detect. Researchers and institutions, for their part, could lose credibility if hypothetical applications were presented as accomplished work.

How we would address this

We would present our methods and results plainly, limitations and unsuccessful experiments included. We would also make clear what specific results can be actually obtained from the gel.
