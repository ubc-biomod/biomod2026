Introduction and Diagnostic Motivation

1.1 Background and Diagnostic Motivation

Tumor-based cancer mortality is heavily driven by metastasis, the process by which primary tumors alter their phenotypic state and colonize distant organs. A central driver of metastasis is the Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT), a dynamic cellular process during highly organized epithelial cells transition into motile, invasive mesenchymal cells.

We chose VIM and CDH1 as targets because they are good markers found in cancers for the mesenchymal and epithelial states (see Wetlab section). Measuring the relative abundance of CDH1 and VIM mRNA provides a vital molecular portrait of a tumor's invasive state and metastatic potential.

To create an accessible, low-cost diagnostic platform capable of quantifying these biomarker shifts, our system targets RNA transcripts directly. We made this decision over choosing targeting proteins or DNA. Protein-based assays are much more costly, relying on expensive equipment. Furthermore, DNA abundance would not change with EMT. Because nearly every cell in a patient's body contains the exact same genomic DNA sequence and copy number for CDH1 and VIM, measuring DNA would not reveal the state of a tumor cell.

1.2 Target Selection & Biological Specificity

Full length RNA sequences are extremely large (the CDH1 mRNA spans ~4,500 nucleotides and the VIM mRNA spans ~2,100 nucleotides). Attempting to target an entire mRNA transcript would present significant structural and financial challenges.

Long mRNA strands self-fold into dense, highly stable secondary and tertiary structures composed of internal hairpins, loops, and double-stranded stems. This would cause much of the target region to be inaccessible for binding. Additionally, chemical synthesis of full-length or long RNA probes is commercially unfeasible and cost-prohibitive for a point-of-care diagnostic (Mak et al., 2016; Schliekelman et al., 2015).

As a result, we made the decision to standardize probe targeting to a concise 20 nucleotide sequence length for both CDH1 and VIM for two reasons:

  1. Natural Biomarker Fragmentation: Extracellular cell-free RNA (cfRNA) in circulating liquid biopsies does not exist intact; systemic nucleases rapidly degrade full-length transcripts into short, protected 20-80 nt fragments.
  2. Thermodynamic Specificity & Accessibility: A 20 nt window provides sufficient statistical uniqueness to prevent off-target binding across the human transcriptome ( possibilities) while remaining structurally unstructured and accessible for rapid, room-temperature strand displacement.

How we plan to visualize results

2.1 Native PAGE as an Accessible Diagnostic Readout

To translate RNA abundance into a clear visual signal, our design uses native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). This was mainly a cost-driven decision and we are targeting LIC/LMIC areas.

Raw polyacrylamide gel matrix costs fractions of a cent per test, eliminating the need for expensive fluorophores, quencher modifications, or specialized sequencing instruments. Additionally, PAGE runs on basic, portable power supplies and standard transilluminators, enabling field deployment without expensive capital equipment (Cunha et al., 2020).

2.2 Effects of Branching vs Linear Mass on Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (PAGE)

Traditional gel electrophoresis separates molecules based on mass and charge. However, our readout mechanism leverages topological shifts/branching and conformational geometry.

Linear nucleic acids migrate efficiently through gel matrix pores via end-first reptation, whereas branched structures, such as the -junctions formed upon target hybridization present multiple arms simultaneously at pore intersections. This topological constraint geometrically pins the junction vertex at matrix pores, dramatically reducing electrophoretic mobility regardless of the charge-to-mass ratio. Building on the principles established by Heuer et al. (2003, 2004), increasing the number and asymmetry of rigid branches directly slows migration on native polyacrylamide gels. We designed our detector strand to exploit this structural behavior to yield visually distinct bands based on target presence for our three conformations:

  • Conformation Z (CDH1 target only): Forms a partially branched complex with the fewest extraneous branches, migrating the fastest through the gel matrix
  • Conformation Y (Both VIM and CDH1 targets present): Assembles a fully branched 3-way -junction complex, exhibiting intermediate migration speed due to branch pinning at gel pores
  • Conformation X (VIM target only): Forms a distinct, highly asymmetric branched complex that experiences maximum topological retardation, migrating the slowest on native PAGE.

3.2 Biological Annotation

Top candidate regions identified by the sliding-window scanner were manually cross-referenced against NCBI transcript annotations and evaluated in NUPACK to inspect ensemble complex formation.

Biological context was carefully screened to verify whether candidate sequences overlapped exons, untranslated regions (UTRs), or critical protein domains. For example, several CDH1 candidate sequences mapped to exons encoding E-cadherin domains responsible for binding -, -, and -catenins or CTNND1 and PSEN1. While these functionally conserved regions confirmed target sequence stability, manual filtering ensured our selected 20 nt window balanced sequence conservation with target accessibility.

3.3 detector Strand Design

Once we confirmed what our VIM and CDH1 target regions were, a detector strand was designed to control the conformational geometry of our conditional conformations.

To ensure our design forms a defined branched structure upon target binding, we engineered hairpin loops (specifically stable GNRA tetraloops) into the detector strand architecture. Incorporating a central hairpin loop serves two vital structural functions:

  • Enforcing Branch Geometry: The hairpin stem acts as a rigid hinge, forcing the detector strand to fold back on itself so that bound target fragments extend outward as distinct -junction branches rather than floppy, unpredictable coils.

  • Preventing Off-Target Folding: The stable stem-loop sequesters internal bases in the unreacted detector strand, preventing non-specific self-folding or homodimerization and ensuring that conformational shifts occur strictly in response to target binding.

4.2 Detector Strand

To determine the optimal hinge geometry for conditional branching, we evaluated multiple linker lengths and loop motifs using NUPACK tube simulations. We confirmed that a single, highly stable complex matching the exact predicted 3-way -junction structure was formed for each conformation. Each combination of target presence (VIM, CDH1, or both) produced a clean, unique secondary structure.

Finally, simulations were done using SimRNA 3D structure predictions to validate our conformations and how they would behave in a gel.

Figure 4.2.2: Simulated 3D shape of each conformation done with SimRNA.

Our SimRNA 3D tertiary structure simulations directly validate our core design hypothesis: targeted RNA hybridization forces our engineered helper strand to undergo a conformational shift into distinct 3D branching topologies, translating molecular recognition into sterically differentiated electrophoretic readouts.

In Conformation Z (CDH1 bound), SimRNA modeling confirms that the helper strand forms a single linear duplex stem with the 20 nt CDH1 fragment while keeping the unreacted VIM binding domain sequestered in a compact loop hinge. This streamlined geometry presents a minimal cross-section, allowing the molecule to navigate polyacrylamide gel pores via unhindered end-first reptation and validating why Conformation Z exhibits the fastest electrophoretic mobility.

Conversely, Conformation X (VIM bound) forces the central 6 bp GNRA tetraloop hinge to bulge outward as an asymmetric 3D hairpin. This creates a rigid structural kink between the extended VIM duplex stem and the unreacted CDH1 arm, generating high steric hindrance that explains the slowest electrophoretic migration observed on native PAGE.

Most importantly, Conformation Y (CDH1 + VIM bound) successfully assembles into a fully branched, rigid 3-way -junction star. Anchored by the central GNRA tetraloop hinge, both target arms project outward at distinct rigid angles. This 3D tertiary topology confirms the physical mechanism established by Heuer et al. (2003, 2004): presenting three rigid duplex arms simultaneously creates a central vertex that geometrically pins at polyacrylamide matrix pores, dramatically retarding mobility independently of mass and confirming an intermediate, highly distinct band migration.

Together, these 3D tertiary models demonstrate that our helper strand reliably translates biomarker binding into distinct structural states suitable for low-cost PAGE readouts.

4.3 Robustness & Edge-Case Verification

To prove that our diagnostic platform remains effective under non-ideal biological conditions, we reused and adapted our sliding-window thermodynamic scanner to computationally evaluate the impact of "messy" circulating RNA fragments.

In real liquid biopsies, extracellular nucleases do not yield clean, perfectly intact 20 nt target sequences. Instead, circulating transcripts exhibit cleavage fluctuations, producing variable lengths (e.g., 18–21 nt), coordinate frame shifts ( or  nt offsets), or single-nucleotide mutations/edits.

How We Reused the Sliding-Window Workflow
Rather than pairing each target window against its own dynamic reverse complement (which would change both strands simultaneously), we anchored the clean 20 nt target’s complement sequence as a fixed reference partner. We then generated systematic perturbations of the candidate strand (CDH1 and VIM) and evaluated them against this fixed partner.

Conclusion

This project presents a computational pipeline for detecting circulating CDH1 and VIM RNA biomarkers using an engineered detector strand architecture. Through sliding-window thermodynamic scanning and domain filtering, we selected accessible, conserved 20 nt target sequences. Incorporating a central 6 bp GNRA tetraloop hinge into the detector strand successfully enforces a stable 3-way -junction complex upon simultaneous target binding, enabling clear band resolution on native PAGE. Finally, our messy-fragment analysis verified that the system maintains high thermodynamic stability and core duplex structure across real-world length variations, frame shifts, and single-base mutations.
